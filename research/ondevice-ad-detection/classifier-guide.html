The ad line classifier

A small text model that scores every transcript line with how likely it is to be part of an ad. It is too rough to skip ads by itself, but as a second opinion on the LLM it removes false alarms without losing real ad breaks.

Where it sits in the pipeline

The LLM (Gemma via LiteRT-LM) does the real work: it copies each ad out of the transcript, and a verify call double-checks it. The classifier runs next to it on the same transcript and gets a veto: an LLM detection is kept only if the classifier also sees something ad-like in that stretch of audio.

How it works

  1. Start from transcript lines

    Moonshine's voice-activity detector splits the audio into speech segments, and neighbouring segments are merged into lines of at most 25 words that never span a pause longer than 1.5 s. A one-hour episode becomes roughly 400 to 600 lines, each with a start and end time.

  2. Turn each line into word features

    Each line is described by the words and word pairs it contains, using TF-IDF: a word counts more when it is frequent in this line and rare across all lines. This is done twice, once for the line itself and once for a five-line context window (two lines before and two after), so a line inherits some signal from its neighbours. Words that appear in fewer than two lines are dropped. That gives about 80,000 features.

  3. Score the line with logistic regression

    The model has one weight per feature. A line's score is the sum of the weights of its features, squashed into a probability between 0 and 1. Positive weights push towards "ad", negative weights towards "content". Training sets the weights from labelled episodes: about 4,800 lines, of which about 480 are ad lines. Ads are only one line in ten, so the training gives ad lines extra weight (class_weight="balanced"). Self-promo lines, which are neither clearly ad nor content, are left out of training.

  4. Smooth and threshold

    Single lines are noisy, so each score is averaged with the line before and after. A line counts as ad when the smoothed score is above 0.15. The threshold is low on purpose: as a veto, the classifier should almost never miss a real ad, and the LLM already provides precision.

  5. Turn lines into time ranges

    Consecutive ad lines become one interval, intervals less than 20 s apart are merged, and anything shorter than 10 s is dropped. This is the same conversion the LLM pipeline uses.

  6. Use it as a veto

    For each ad break the LLM reports, check whether any classifier interval overlaps it. If none does, drop the LLM's detection. The classifier never adds breaks of its own.

What it learned

These are the strongest weights from a model trained on twelve episodes (Daily 2 held out). The ad side is web addresses and calls to action. The content side is mostly past-tense storytelling and pronouns, which is how interviews and true-crime narration sound.

"october" ranks high because several held-out ads promote films and TV shows opening in October. This is the classifier's main weakness: part of what it learns is this season's sponsors, not ads in general.

How well it does

Each episode was scored by a model trained on the other twelve (leave-one-episode-out), so no episode is scored by a model that saw it. F1 combines precision (how much of the flagged time is really ads) and recall (how much of the ad time is found); 1.0 is perfect.

On its own the classifier finds every ad break but flags too much content, so its time ranges are too loose to skip by. As a veto it never cost a real break in any test and removed half the remaining false alarms.

Running it on the phone

  • No ML runtime needed. Scoring is a vocabulary lookup, a weighted sum and a sigmoid, which is a few dozen lines of Kotlin. The model ships as a word-to-weight table plus the IDF values.
  • Size. The full model pickles to about 2.5 MB. Keeping only the 5,000 to 10,000 strongest features should bring it to a few hundred KB with little loss, though that is not measured yet.
  • Speed. Scoring an hour of transcript should take well under a second on a phone CPU (sparse dot products; not yet measured on a device), which is negligible next to transcription and the LLM.
  • Training happens offline on a computer. The weights are exported once and bundled with the app.

Limits and caveats

  • Small training set. 13 episodes, 55 ad breaks, about 11 hours of audio. The weights partly memorise this month's sponsors (YouTube Premium, BetterHelp, films opening in October), so the classifier will drift as sponsors change and needs retraining with new labelled episodes.
  • Threshold picked by looking at results. The 0.15 threshold was chosen after seeing held-out scores at 0.15, 0.25 and 0.35, and the leave-one-episode-out numbers above use the same value. That makes them a little optimistic.
  • Words only. It cannot hear music, pauses or voices, and it reads ASR errors as they are. A host-read ad with no web address and no "sponsored by" can slip under the threshold.
  • Possible upgrade. Replacing word counts with sentence embeddings, for example from EmbeddingGemma (300M parameters, available for LiteRT), should generalise better to sponsors the model has not seen, at the cost of a small extra model.
